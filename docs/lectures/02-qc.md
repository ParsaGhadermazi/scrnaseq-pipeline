## 1. Two different kinds of operation

Everything here is called "QC", but the steps split cleanly in two:

| Step | Removes cells? | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| `emptyDrops` | **yes** | drops barcodes with no cell in them |
| SoupX / DecontX | **no** | rewrites counts *inside* every surviving cell |
| scDblFinder | **yes** | drops barcodes holding 2+ cells |
| cell QC | **yes** | drops barcodes holding 1 *damaged* cell |

The three removal steps partition one question — **what is in this droplet?**

- **0 cells** → empty droplet → `emptyDrops`
- **2+ cells** → doublet → `scDblFinder`
- **1 cell, dying** → low quality → QC thresholds

Orthogonally to all three: **every droplet, including the good ones, is
contaminated with ambient RNA**, because free mRNA was floating in the
suspension and entered every GEM. That is why ambient correction operates on the
survivors rather than removing anyone.

## 2. Why cells go bad, and what reveals it

The root event is **plasma membrane rupture** during dissociation. It has two
consequences: the escaped RNA becomes the **ambient soup**, and the damaged cell
itself still gets captured and lands in your matrix.

**Mitochondria are the tell.** They have their own genome (13 protein-coding
genes, `MT-*`), their own double membrane, and are far too large to drain out
through holes in the plasma membrane. As cytoplasmic mRNA leaks away,
mitochondrial transcripts stay.

> The crucial subtlety: **mitochondrial counts do not go up.** They stay
> constant while everything else drains. The *denominator* shrinks.

Hence a fraction, not a count: `percent.mt = MT- UMIs / total UMIs`. A cell at
40% mito is not making more mitochondrial RNA — it has lost most of everything
else.

**Two things `percent.mt < 10%` hides:**

- **It is tissue-dependent.** 10% is a PBMC convention. Cardiomyocytes,
  hepatocytes and skeletal muscle are genuinely mitochondria-dense; 30–50% can be
  healthy. The tutorial gives PBMC <10%, brain <5%, tumour <20%.
- **In single-*nucleus* data the logic inverts.** Nuclei should contain no
  mitochondria, so high `percent.mt` means *cytoplasmic contamination*, not death.

Also tracked: `percent.ribo` (`^RPS`/`^RPL`) and haemoglobin (`^HB`).

## 3. nFeature and nCount, and the trap in the upper bound

- **Very low** `nFeature` → either an empty droplet or a ruptured cell. These
  look identical in `nFeature` alone; **`percent.mt` separates them** — a dying
  cell retains mitochondria, an ambient-only droplet just has a dilute copy of
  the soup's composition. This is why the metrics are read jointly
  (`FeatureScatter`), never in isolation.
- **Very high** `nFeature`/`nCount` → a doublet, **or a genuinely big cell**.

That second possibility is the trap:

> **A fixed upper count threshold preferentially deletes your most
> transcriptionally active cell types.**

Plasma cells, megakaryocytes, activated macrophages and proliferating cells
genuinely carry several times the RNA of a resting lymphocyte — and they are
usually why the experiment was run. Worse, they vanish silently: absent from the
clusters, not flagged as removed.

This is exactly why doublets are detected by **profile**, not magnitude. A
T-cell/monocyte doublet expresses `CD3D` *and* `CD14`, a combination no real cell
shows. Magnitude cannot separate "two cells" from "one big cell"; profile can.

**The hard limit:** this only works for **heterotypic** doublets. A **homotypic**
doublet — two T cells — has a normal T-cell profile at roughly double the counts,
and is very nearly undetectable. Every tool in the coverage matrix is largely
blind to them, and that residual is carried into the results.

## 4. Thresholds: fixed vs adaptive

The tutorial sets thresholds by eye from violin plots and hardcodes them
(`nFeature` 500–5000, `nCount` 800–20000, `percent.mt` <10%), arguing explicitly
for "visual inspection... rather than relying on automatic statistical methods".

Two things break that on a second dataset:

- **Tissue**, as above.
- **Sequencing depth.** Sequence twice as deeply and every cell's `nCount`
  roughly doubles. `nFeature` is more forgiving because gene detection saturates,
  but a hardcoded count window silently encodes how deep you sequenced.

### Why mean ± 3 SD is the wrong fix

**The outliers you are hunting are inside the statistic you use to find them.**
SD squares deviations, so extreme cells inflate it, which widens the gate, which
lets them through. The mean is dragged toward them at the same time. The dirtier
the sample, the more permissive the filter — exactly backwards.

### MAD

```
MAD = median(|x − median(x)|)
flag if |x − median| / MAD > n          n = 3 (strict) or 5 (permissive)
```

Worked example — five ordinary cells and one catastrophic one, `[10,11,12,13,14,200]`:

| | mean/SD | median/MAD |
|---|---|---|
| centre | 43.3 | 12.5 |
| spread | 76.8 | 1.5 |
| ±3 gate | [−187, 273] | [8, 17] |
| flags the 200? | **no** | **yes** |

Breakdown point: SD 0%, median and MAD 50%.

**Scaling constant, a real Python/R gotcha.** MAD ≈ 0.6745σ for normal data, so
it is scaled by **1.4826** to be SD-comparable. `R: mad(x)` applies it by
default; `scipy.stats.median_abs_deviation(x)` does **not** — you need
`scale="normal"`. Same concept, thresholds ~48% apart.

**Two refinements we implement:**

- **Log-transform the counts first.** `nCount`/`nFeature` are right-skewed; a
  symmetric ±n rule on a skewed distribution cuts far too much off one side.
- **Mitochondria need a hybrid.** In a very clean sample MAD is tiny and 3 MADs
  might cut at 4%; in a badly dissociated one the median itself is high. So:
  MAD bound **and** an absolute ceiling, whichever is stricter.

**The edge case that bites:** **MAD can be exactly 0** when >50% of values are
identical — routine for `percent.mt` in single-nucleus data. Then the rule
divides by zero or flags everything. `cell_qc.py` detects this, skips filtering
on that metric, and says so rather than silently deleting the sample.

## 5. How ambient correction actually works

Contamination is only measurable **where you know the true value should be zero**,
and clusters are what make that knowable.

Group cells by type, then find genes strongly expressed in some clusters and
biologically absent from others:

> `HBB` is haemoglobin. Erythrocytes make it; T cells do not. Every `HBB` count
> in a T cell is, by construction, **pure soup**.

Compare that against the soup profile measured from the empty droplets, and the
ratio gives the contamination fraction ρ. `autoEstCont()` automates finding such
genes; `setClusters()` is what makes "should be zero" groups identifiable.

**SoupX vs DecontX** (Part 2-2): both need clusters. SoupX estimates a **global
ρ** and subtracts it everywhere — transparent and conservative, needs the raw
matrix. DecontX fits a Bayesian mixture and gives a **per-cell** estimate,
usable when only the filtered matrix exists.

## 6. Ordering — the part the tutorial never addresses

Each step changes what the next one sees.

**Ambient inflates counts — it never subtracts.** So filtering on uncorrected
counts is **too permissive**: a dying cell propped up by soup sails over an
`nFeature >= 500` floor.

But soup volume depends on droplet volume, not cell size, so a small cell
receives the same *absolute* soup as a large one — a much larger *fraction* of
its counts. After correction, small cells lose proportionally more. Filter with
thresholds chosen before correction and you cut genuinely small healthy cells.
Another argument for MAD computed **on the corrected matrix**.

**And there is a loop.** SoupX needs clusters → clustering needs normalisation →
but normalisation belongs after correction. Resolved with a throwaway
preliminary clustering:

```
1. load raw + filtered
2. emptyDrops on raw
3. PRELIMINARY normalize -> HVG -> PCA -> cluster     <- discarded; only labels SoupX
4. SoupX(tod=raw, toc=cells) + setClusters(step 3)
5. doublet detection      on corrected counts
6. cell QC                on corrected counts
7. gene QC                min-cells, drop HB
8. REAL normalize -> HVG
```

Steps 3 and 8 are the same operations, run twice, on different data, for
different purposes. Steps 5 and 6 could legitimately swap — the tutorial does
doublets first, but dying cells make the kNN neighbourhood scDblFinder scores
against noisy. We follow the tutorial and expose it.

## 7. Normalisation

Two cells: A has 20,000 UMIs with 200 `CD3D`; B has 2,000 with 40. Raw counts say
A has **5× more**. As fractions: A 1.0%, B 2.0% — B expresses it at **twice** A's
rate. The raw comparison returns the opposite biological answer.

Cluster on raw counts and **PC1 is essentially total UMI count**; your clusters
separate deep cells from shallow ones. The result looks clean and is pure
artifact.

```
LogNormalize:  log(1 + count / total_counts × 10000)
```

- **divide by the cell total** — fixes the above
- **×10,000** — "counts per 10K"; bulk uses CPM (1e6), single cells hold far
  fewer molecules
- **log1p** — stabilises variance (in count data variance grows with the mean, so
  a few loud genes would dominate every PC) and makes changes multiplicative.
  `+1` because the matrix is 95% zeros and `log(0)` is `-Inf`.

### What it costs

Dividing by the total assumes the total is **technical**. It is partly
biological. After division you no longer have expression — you have
**proportions**. Two cells with identical composition but tenfold different RNA
content become numerically identical, so global transcriptional amplification
(`MYC`-driven tumour cells, activated lymphocytes, S-phase cells) is invisible.

**Worse, it fabricates signal.** In a plasma cell, immunoglobulin can be 30–40%
of the library. Divide by the total and *every other gene* is divided by an
inflated denominator, so all of them look **down-regulated** — confident,
entirely spurious differential expression. This is **composition bias**, and it
is why bulk RNA-seq uses DESeq2's median-of-ratios and edgeR's TMM instead.

Alternatives: `scran` pooling size factors, **SCTransform** (regularised NB
regression, Pearson residuals), analytic Pearson residuals. The field has not
settled this; LogNormalize remains the common default and is the tutorial's choice.

## 8. HVG selection

~2,000 of ~30,000 genes are kept. Not a loss:

- **Most genes carry no identity information** — unexpressed here, or uniform
  across all cells (`ACTB`, `GAPDH`, ribosomal proteins). A gene identical in all
  10,000 cells cannot distinguish them; it only adds noise to every distance.
- **Curse of dimensionality.** In 30,000 dimensions distances converge and the
  kNN graph clustering depends on stops being meaningful.

`vst` does something subtler than "highest variance": variance scales with the
mean in count data, so ranking by raw variance would just return the most highly
expressed genes. It fits a loess of `log(variance)` on `log(mean)`, standardises
by the expected variance, and ranks the standardised values — selecting genes
**more variable than expected for their expression level**. That is how a lowly
expressed cell-type marker beats a loud housekeeping gene.

Python equivalent: `sc.pp.highly_variable_genes(flavor="seurat_v3")` — and note
it expects **raw counts**, unlike `flavor="seurat"`. Wrong matrix, silently
different gene set.

### The batch trap

Run HVG selection on pooled multi-sample data and the most "variable" genes
become the ones that differ *between* samples:

- **donor** — `HLA` (highly polymorphic), and `XIST`/`RPS4Y1`/`DDX3Y`/`UTY` if
  donors differ in sex, which makes cells cluster by donor sex
- **dissociation stress** — `FOS`, `JUN`, `JUNB`, `EGR1`, `HSPA1A`, `HSP90AA1`
  are induced by the enzymatic digestion itself, so *how long a sample sat in
  collagenase* becomes a top axis of variation

HVGs define PCA → PCA defines the kNN graph → the graph defines clusters. Your
clusters become **samples**, not cell types.

Fix: compute variability **per sample**. Seurat v5 does this with split layers;
scanpy takes `batch_key="sample"`. A gene must be variable *within* samples, not
merely *between* them.
